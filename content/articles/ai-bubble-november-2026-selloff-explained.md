Within eight days in November 2026, AI-related stocks absorbed two shocks: a broad selloff after the US midterm elections, and the resignation of Nvidia's chief executive. The phrase "AI bubble" is back in every headline.

This piece explains what happened, why debt is the part to watch, and what a correction would and would not change for people who use AI.

## What happened?

On 5 November, technology stocks fell hard. The Nasdaq Composite dropped by more than 5 percent, and reports put the value wiped out at around 2.5 trillion dollars in a day. Chipmakers took the worst of it, with AMD and Micron each down roughly 15 percent and Nvidia down close to 9 percent.

A week later, Nvidia announced that Jensen Huang had resigned, sending its shares and those of AI cloud providers down again. We cover that separately in [what Huang's resignation means for the AI chip market](/articles/nvidia-ceo-jensen-huang-resigns-what-it-means/).

## What triggered it?

Not a bad earnings report or a failed product. The trigger was political.

The midterm results raised the prospect of tougher rules on data center construction: power use, water, local approvals. Investors had priced AI companies on the assumption that capacity would keep being built at speed. Anything that slows construction slows revenue.

Geography sharpens the risk. One bank estimate cited in coverage puts more than a quarter of planned US data center capacity in a single state, Texas. Concentration like that means one policy change can matter a great deal.

## Why is debt the real story?

For the first phase of the AI boom, the largest companies paid for data centers out of their own profits. That has changed. Much of the newer capacity is financed with borrowed money, often by smaller specialist companies with long contracts and thin margins.

Reporting this month shows lenders getting more cautious. Bond investors are asking for higher returns to fund AI projects, and some borrowers have had to offer yields closer to those of risky debt to get deals done.

That matters because of how booms end. Falling share prices hurt investors. Debt that cannot be refinanced closes companies. One research firm cited in coverage estimates that a serious unwinding could take at least 30 percent off US share prices and produce credit losses in the hundreds of billions of dollars. Other analysts see a slow release of pressure instead of a collapse, and argue that a real crash would need AI spending to stop outright, which is not what they expect.

## Is this like the dot-com crash?

There are similarities and one large difference.

| | Dot-com, 2000 | AI, 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Real technology underneath | Yes | Yes |
| Infrastructure built ahead of demand | Yes, fibre | Yes, data centers |
| Leading companies profitable | Mostly not | Mostly yes |
| Financed by debt | Heavily, in telecoms | Increasingly |
| Paying customers today | Few | Many |

The difference is that today's largest AI spenders have enormous existing profits. The similarity is the pattern of building capacity on borrowed money before demand is proven. After 2000 the internet did not go away. Many of the companies did.

## What would a correction change for AI users?

Less than the headlines suggest, but not nothing.

- **The tools keep working.** Models that exist continue to exist. Open-weight models you can run yourself cannot be switched off by a market. See our guide to the [top open-source LLMs](/articles/top-open-source-llms/).
- **Prices may rise.** A lot of AI is sold below cost to win users. Tighter money ends subsidies: smaller free tiers, higher prices, stricter limits.
- **Smaller vendors are at risk.** A startup that depends on cheap capital and a single model provider may not survive. If your business relies on one, have an exit plan.
- **The pace of new capacity slows.** That could mean longer waits for the most capable models and compute.

## What should a business do?

1. **Avoid lock-in.** Keep your prompts, data and workflows portable between providers.
2. **Check your vendors.** Ask how they are funded and what happens to your data if they close.
3. **Budget on value, not hype.** Projects with a measurable return survive a downturn. Our guide to [AI automation for business](/articles/ai-automation-for-business/) covers how to pick them.
4. **Do not confuse stock prices with usefulness.** Whether a tool saves your team time is unrelated to what its maker's shares did this week.

## The takeaway

A market correction and a technology failing are different things. The first may well be under way. The second is not on the evidence so far: usage is real and growing. The sensible position is to keep using what works, and to make sure nothing important to you depends on a company that only survives if money stays cheap.
