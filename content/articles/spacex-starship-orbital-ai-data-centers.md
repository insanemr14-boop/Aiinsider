Three things that sound unrelated are now one strategy. SpaceX owns an AI company. It has asked regulators for permission to launch up to a million satellites that would act as data centers. And this week it is attempting to catch both stages of its Starship rocket for the first time.

Together they amount to a bet that the cheapest place to run AI will eventually be orbit. This article explains the plan and the problems.

## How did SpaceX end up in AI?

SpaceX confirmed in early 2026 that it had acquired xAI, the AI company behind the Grok assistant. The business now sits inside SpaceX as a subsidiary. In June 2026 SpaceX listed its shares in what was reported as the largest initial public offering ever, raising about 86 billion dollars.

The stated reason for combining a rocket company and an AI lab was compute. AI needs vast amounts of power, and SpaceX believes it can supply that power from orbit.

## What is the orbital data center plan?

SpaceX has asked the US Federal Communications Commission to authorize up to **one million satellites** designed as orbital data centers. According to coverage of the filing, each would:

- Generate power from solar panels, which receive near-constant sunlight in the right orbit.
- Cool itself by radiating heat into space, with no water.
- Operate for about five years before being replaced.

Reported targets are demonstration launches in late 2027 and commercial service around 2028.

## Why would anyone compute in space?

Because the limits on Earth are no longer chips. They are power, land, water and permission.

Building a large data center now means waiting years for a grid connection and negotiating over water use and local opposition. Those constraints were a central cause of this month's fall in AI stocks, as we explain in [the November 2026 selloff](/articles/ai-bubble-november-2026-selloff-explained/). Orbit has none of them. Sunlight is free and continuous, and nobody lives next door.

## Why does Starship matter?

The whole idea depends on the cost of getting mass to orbit. Computing hardware, solar panels and radiators are heavy. At historical launch prices, orbital data centers make no economic sense.

Starship is meant to change that by being **fully and rapidly reusable**, like an aircraft. That is the significance of **Flight 15**, scheduled for 13 November 2026 from Starbase in Texas. It is the first attempt to catch both stages:

1. The **Super Heavy booster**, caught by the launch tower minutes after liftoff.
2. The **upper stage**, caught roughly 22 hours later after several orbits of the Earth.

SpaceX has caught boosters before, though reports note it has not yet caught one of its latest version. It has never caught an upper stage. At the time of writing the flight had not yet taken place, so treat the outcome as unknown. A failure would be normal for a test programme. A success would be the strongest evidence yet that full reuse is achievable.

## What are the hard problems?

SpaceX's own filings list the commercial viability of orbital AI computing as a risk. The engineering reasons are real:

| Problem | Why it is hard |
|---|---|
| Heat | In a vacuum there is no air or water to carry heat away. Radiators must be very large. |
| Radiation | Cosmic radiation damages chips and causes errors. Shielding adds mass. |
| Repairs | Nothing can be fixed by hand. A failed part means a failed satellite. |
| Latency | Signals take time to travel. Fine for training, worse for real-time use. |
| Data transfer | Moving huge datasets up and down needs very high-capacity links. |
| Debris | A million more satellites raises collision risk and regulatory resistance. |
| Hardware cycles | AI chips are outdated in a few years, matching the short satellite lifespan but making each launch a recurring cost. |

## What does this mean for AI?

For the next few years, nothing changes for most users. The AI you use will run in buildings on the ground.

The importance is strategic. If launch costs fall far enough, energy stops being the limit on how much AI can be built. That would reshape who can afford to train the largest models, a question we return to in our [AI trends analysis](/articles/ai-trends-2026/). If they do not fall, orbital computing stays a niche for specialised workloads.

Either way, watch the rocket, not the announcements. The business case for AI in orbit is a launch-cost equation, and Starship reuse is the variable that decides it.
